Press and Type

The merge of C and D held up, but the button row didn't: the spacing read as random. It was — three separate pieces of arithmetic, none of them deliberate. This revision fixes the geometry first, then shows the three ways left to express it.

Why it looked random three bugs, not a taste problem

The old row and the new one, each with a ruler under it marking where the four items actually begin.

1

45 doesn't divide by four. Equal quarters of 11 leave a stray column, so the row never reached the right edge.

2

The keys aren't the same width. ⏎ and → are one cell; ^x and ^t are two. Anything placed after the key starts in two different columns.

3

The labels aren't the same length. Left-aligning scatters the right edges; centring scatters the left ones. Either way the eye finds no interval.

gutter 1 + ( cell 10 + separator 1 ) × 4 = 45  ·  key right-aligned in a 2-cell slot, cap always 4 wide, label always at cell + 4

The cap is the anchor, not the label: pad every key to two cells and right-align it, and a one-cell ⏎ ends on the same column as a two-cell ^x. Everything after it inherits the rhythm. The tiling has no remainder, so the row starts one column in and ends one column from the edge — the same margins the field and the count row use.

E1 in the states that matter

Nothing typed, a toggle on, the whole screen, the same screen with no colour, and the keyboard layout where there is no button row at all.

What I would build

E1, on the fixed tiling. The asymmetry was never a case for more chrome — it was three arithmetic mistakes wearing a taste problem's clothes. With the caps on a rhythm, the plain row reads as four buttons without a single divider or fill, and the surface stays quiet enough to sit under a list.

E2 and E3 remain one constant away each: a separator glyph, or a cell width that swallows its own padding. If the row reads as ambiguous on the real screen rather than merely quiet, E2 is the smaller step.

One consequence for the code: the hit test currently divides the width by four. It has to use the same tiling as the renderer, or a tap near a boundary lands on the neighbour — one function, called by both halves, the way the row-height arithmetic already works.
